HPV-associated oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) now makes up most oropharyngeal cancer in the United States. Patients are younger and live longer, so the long-term toxicity of treatment matters more. Treating both sides of the neck, whether with dissection or radiation, adds dysphagia, xerostomia, and feeding-tube dependence. Treating only the ipsilateral neck spares the opposite-side salivary and swallowing structures.

For well-lateralized tonsil cancer, ipsilateral radiation is supported by retrospective series and pooled analyses. Pooled contralateral failure is about 2–3% (Al-Mamgani, Oral Oncol 2017; Razavian, JAMA Netw Open 2023). The 2017 ASTRO guideline strongly recommends ipsilateral radiation for T1–2 N0–1 tonsil cancer without soft-palate or base-of-tongue (BOT) involvement. It only conditionally recommends it for patients with multiple ipsilateral nodes (AJCC-7 N2b) or under 1 cm of palate extension (Sher, Pract Radiat Oncol 2017). For N2b disease, reported contralateral failure ranges from 0% to 14% (Frankart 2022; Natesan 2023; KROG 11-07; Maskell 2019), and multiple nodes and extranodal extension have been linked to contralateral recurrence (Lynch, Oral Oncol 2014). An international survey found that nearly all clinicians treat small node-negative lateralized tonsil cancers ipsilaterally. With soft-palate or BOT extension, midline proximity, or a higher nodal burden, however, most radiation oncologists treat both necks. This reflects uncertainty about the true contralateral risk (Oral Oncol 2021). The Canadian Cancer Trials Group HN.11 (SELECT, NCT05451004) trial is testing whether SPECT-CT lymphatic mapping can safely select which lateralized cancers need contralateral neck treatment.

Pathologic data from the contralateral neck are the most direct measure of occult disease. In HPV-positive BOT cancers treated with transoral surgery and bilateral neck dissection, occult contralateral metastasis was 21% (Last, JAMA Otolaryngol 2020). In cT1–2 cohorts it ranged from 2% to 13%, associated with clinical N category, multiple ipsilateral nodes, and extranodal extension (Yao, Head Neck 2021; Kornfeld 2024). No published series has reported contralateral pathologic nodal status by the number of ipsilateral positive nodes together with measured palate or BOT extension. Nor has any study measured how reliably clinicians judge "palate involvement" or "BOT involvement," even though these judgments drive the decision to treat the opposite neck.

Since 2015, Northwestern has treated more than 250 patients with OPSCC with primary transoral surgery. About 75–80 of them had bilateral neck dissection for BOT primary, BOT or palate extension, or an equivocal contralateral node. This cohort provides pathologic ground truth for the contralateral neck in exactly the scenarios where practice is most divided. The remaining patients, treated with ipsilateral dissection, show contralateral failure rates according to the adjuvant radiation fields actually delivered. These results will add to the evidence base on which HN.11 and future de-escalation strategies rest.
